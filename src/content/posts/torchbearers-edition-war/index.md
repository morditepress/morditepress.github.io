Please forgive the provocative title, a blogger's gotta blog.

It's been a few years now since Torchbearer 2e released. Many people enjoy it. Some found it divisive. I found it inconvenient at first, then ultimately destructive for Mordite Press.

Whenever the topic is broached on Ross' excellent torchbearer podcast, *Born to Lose*, discussion of the rift seems confusing to 2e fans.

1e advocates are thought to be reactionary, resisting the new edition simply because they don't like change, or because they're committed to a D&D nostalgia that's now out of fashion. It doesn't help that there *are* some folks out there who're resistant to change, or did find D&D nostalgia appealing. I've read some of those posts. The quality varies.

**But there are legitimate reasons that some folks who love 1e couldn't summon the same enthusiasm for 2e, and I'd like to get into them here.**

I think that the folks at Burning Wheel and their players should hear these reasons articulated by someone who knows the game intimately. I certainly don't expect them to change their approach; there are many disagreements between us, but their creative integrity is not at issue. It's their game, they should make the game they want.

Likewise, in defiance of this post's title, it's OK if you like Torchbearer 2nd edition. There's a lot that I like about it, too— but, as I'll explain, it would have landed better with me if BW had been more considerate of the community and collaborators they had gathered. It's still a unique game; a great game. But it has, for me, smothered the game we had.

>[!attention]+ An Elephant In the Room
*I'll not address other controversies beyond the game's design here. I'm aware of them. I've been adversely affected by their fallout. I could probably add a few more, myself. But this post will focus exclusively on the content of the games. It's also worth noting that Mordite Press is not directly affiliated with Burning Wheel. We currently hold no contract with them beyond our use of the Torchbearer Sagas open license. We've made non-sagas books and will do so again in the future.*

## Blank Slate vs. Detailed Setting
Probably the biggest change between editions was the presentation of the game worlds:

* **TB1** is a rather anonymous dungeons & dragons pastiche, with the specifics of setting charmingly elided in a way that was suggestive of any number of homebrews. It was a "setting" after the manner of B/X, an era where formal campaign settings were just appearing, and yet there were coherent patterns in the world that followed from the rules themselves.
* **TB2** is explicitly set in Middarmark, a campaign setting in the contemporary sense, that richly depicts a world drawing on Norse mythology. It is grim and beautiful, but not without the moments of levity found in TB1. It has specific, named locations. The gods have names. There is a cosmology that is revealed in a top-down manner rather than ground-up.

This wasn't just a change in the fiction of the game. Mechanics changed to suit this new setting. Classes and spell levels changed. In the art of game design, changing these basic mechanics changes the meaning of the game. This was the stated intent. There were things that made BW folks unhappy about clerics and elves, etc., and they made these changes to address those issues.

This might be a lesser concern for players and GMs at the table, but as a publisher under an open license, it means that your work is now deprecated. Even if it's 100% compatible, players will assume it is not and pass you over. If you're in the middle of developing an already-crowdfunded 60-page setting booklet, staying ahead of obsolescence means combing through weekly playtest releases and translating specific norse mythology into your vaguely-comic D&D pastiche. I'll get to that in a bit.

Burning Wheel folks have often asserted that the setting was always Middarmark, and that 2e just reflects a further refinement of those ideas. Fine. They can see it that way. But when I resolved to publish books for the Torchbearer Sagas license, the setting was defined by two sources, and neither of them was the as-yet-unpublished Middarmark. Torchbearer was the 1e rulebook plus Jared Sorensen's terrific expansion classes. Those classes were pure D&D pastiche as well.

That was the game for many years, played weekly (or more) by me and my friends. If it was an accident left by a creative vacuum, so be it. It still means something to me.

## Canon, Anti-canon, and Collaboration
Why am I so fixated on pastiche? Partly because it gave Torchbearer an [anti-canon](https://rpgmuseum.fandom.com/wiki/Anti-canon) space in which to play. As a GM I have always struggled with setting paralysis, where trying to do a detailed setting justice can stop me from running the game. Torchbearer 1e was liberating in that there was nothing to tiptoe around.

But I'm not against detailed settings. Hell, I love them, and I'm guilty of pushing in that direction myself. TB1 seemed to invite me to do so, and TB2 required me to live in Middarmark, or else do considerable work to leave it. Mordite [D. Koch](https://archive.mordite.press/author/d-koch) took that deal and made the [Chalk Horse](https://www.rimholm.com/about) books for Middarmark, which I also enjoy.

BW could easily have left space for multiple settings (and homebrews!) to hook into Torchbearer. I would never begrudge BW putting Middarmark into their game, either. There was a way to do it that didn't invalidate collaborators' published material, and *much* more importantly, existing players' characters and previous participation in the game.

I know because I asked them, repeatedly, not to do things like change spell levels or delete classes when you could just add a new spell, or a new class. They didn't have to deviate from the naming conventions they had gotten so right in the first edition. But ultimately, this wasn't the creative direction they wanted to go. They wanted to change the meaning of the game in specific ways, ways that changed my feelings on the game. Let's talk about that.

## Nostalgia vs. Pastiche
There was something much bigger going on with Torchbearer's D&D pastiche that was lost. It wasn't nostalgia.

**Torchbearer's rules contain powerful value judgements— about the fallacies of heroism and power fantasy, about the nature of violence, and about wealth and success.**

These are all also big concepts in D&D, too, since forever. But in Torchbearer they were each inverted. They were transformed into their opposite.

By being a thoughtfully-designed indie game set in a D&D pastiche, Torchbearer was not nostalgic, *it was a work of artistic criticism*. And in our hobby, there could be no worthier subject of that criticism, and no greater vehicle for it than a game. Ask me why I love Planescape.

So here we're getting into real "forge" stuff (I guess, I wasn't there) — **what's this game about?** Because it seemed to me that this game was about viewing D&D through a lens of 50 years of the hobby's development and a deeper critical understanding of the themes at play. But I guess that was an accident?

You can't just delete D&D from the formula. And for all its many problems, you can't really fix D&D's legacy by editing its mistakes. Many have tried. WotC's still trying. But by distancing itself from the target of its own critique, Torchbearer slipped into a more familiar mode: that of D&D, partially corrected.

## Wither the Picaresque?
One of those incredible moves that TB1 made was to make the adventurers into have-nots. They're not superheroes, and they're not Fantasy Rockstars (to borrow Duamn's term). I think almost all Torchbearer fans respond to that message, perhaps above all others.

But when you swap TB1's reimagined tropes for Middarmark's deep, historically informed setting, you get a different message.

It's superficially the same. You do begin as have-nots.

Torchbearer 2e reverts to the zero-to-hero model of later D&D. Now our downtrodden adventurers are on the path to starting businesses and settlements, buying homes and gaining titles. Instead of being outside the system, they're starting at the bottom and working their way up the ladder of *precedence*. They just need to try hard enough within the system, and life will throw them some curveballs, but they can prevail. Those challenges only test their worthiness on the path to becoming *powerful.*

Sure, it's a less rank message than old *Dungeons & Dragons'* lionized colonialism. But it's still a power fantasy of the same ilk. And that, to me, is a betrayal of what I thought the core theme of Torchbearer was. *Picaresque*. Imperfect people trying to survive by their wits in a world with no place for them.

The shift is compounded by incessant reference to economies and economics, something I find to be exceptionally tasteless in an adventure game. I don't give a single shit about inflation in the local village, and the idea that a GM would be tabulating that stuff in the background is utterly tedious. I don't even think the economics stand up to scrutiny, but I don't hate myself enough to bother proving it.

Likewise war. I can't think of a less appropriate mechanic to introduce to torchbearer than a war sim with protagonists as omniscient generals. Surely we could have said something worthwhile about the hobby here rather than regurgitating its weakest episodes.

**I'm told that you know what a game is about by what it chooses to describe with rules. By this metric, Torchbearer 2e appears to be a game of colonial enterprise, like D&D before it.**

These additions to the game, many of which are not even functional in play, are such a departure from Torchbearer's original appeal that they eroded my interest completely. And boy howdy was I invested in this game.
## Geez, Tell Us How You Really Feel
So where does this leave us? Am I arranging a pyre to burn the TB2 books? No. Games are art, and lots of game-art falls apart under this level of scrutiny. I certainly think it's possible to play 2e and get the old mode out of it, even despite the points above. I don't begrudge the people who still play it. And it's certainly made an impression on me, enough that I have all these thoughts about it. 

So where does this leave us? Am I going to be arranging a pyre to burn the TB2 books? No. Games are art, and lots of game-art falls apart under this level of scrutiny. I certainly think it's possible to play 2e and get the old mode out of it, even despite the points above. I don't begrudge the people who still play it. And it's certainly made an impression on me, enough that I have all these thoughts about it.

But I can't make books for this game anymore, for a host of reasons. Perhaps the loudest reason is that the message that resonated with me just isn't as strong as it was in the first version. But, as I've done with lots of other games, I plan to carry its lessons forward into something new.
