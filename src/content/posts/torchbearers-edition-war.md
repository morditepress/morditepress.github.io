Please forgive the provocative title, a blogger's gotta blog.

It's been a few years now since Torchbearer 2e released. Many people enjoy it. Some found it divisive. I found it inconvenient at first, then existentially destructive for Mordite Press.

Whenever the topic is broached on Ross' excellent torchbearer podcast, *Born to Lose*, discussion of the rift seems confusing to 2e fans. 

1e advocates are thought to be reactionary, resisting the new edition simply because they don't like change, or because they're committed to a D&D nostalgia that's now out of fashion. It doesn't help that there *are* some folks out there who *are* resistant to change, or *did* find D&D nostalgia appealing. I've read some of those posts. The quality varies.

**But there are legitimate reasons that some folks who love 1e couldn't summon the same enthusiasm for 2e, and I'd like to get into them here.**

I think that the folks at Burning Wheel and their players should know the true reasons, articulated by someone who knows the game intimately. I certainly don't expect them to change their approach; there are many disagreements between us, but their creative integrity is not at issue. It's their game, they should make the game they want.

Likewise, in defiance of this post's title, it's OK if you like Torchbearer 2nd edition. There's a lot that I like about it, too— but, as I'll explain, it would have landed better with me if BW had been more considerate of the community and collaborators they had gathered. It's still a unique game; a great game. But it has, for me, smothered the game we had.

>[!attention]+ An Elephant In the Room
*I'll not address other controversies beyond the game's design here. I'm aware of them. I've been adversely affected by their fallout. I could probably add a few more, myself. But this post will focus exclusively on the content of the games. It's also worth noting that Mordite Press is not directly affiliated with Burning Wheel. We currently hold no contract with them beyond our use of the open Torchbearer Sagas license. We've made non-sagas books and will do so again in the future.*

## Blank Slate vs. Detailed Setting 
Probably the biggest change between editions was the presentation of the game worlds: 

* **TB1** is a rather anonymous dungeons & dragons pastiche, with the specifics of setting charmingly elided in a way that was suggestive of any number of homebrews. It was a "setting" after the manner of B/X, an era where formal campaign settings were just appearing, and yet there were coherent patterns in the world that followed from the rules themselves.
* **TB2** is explicitly set in Middarmark, a campaign setting in the contemporary sense, that richly depicts a world drawing on Norse mythology. It is grim and beautiful, but not without the moments of levity found in TB1. It has specific, named locations. The gods have names. There is a cosmology that is revealed in a ground-up manner rather than top-down.

This wasn't just a change in the fiction of the game. Mechanics changed to suit this new setting. Classes and spell names changed. In the art of game design, changing these basic mechanics changes the meaning of the game, full stop.

This might be a lesser concern for players and GMs at the table, but as a publisher under an open license, it means that your work is now deprecated. Even if it's 100% compatible, players will assume it is not and pass you over. If you're in the middle of developing an already-crowdfunded 60-page setting booklet, staying ahead of obsolescence means combing through weekly playtest releases and translating specific norse mythology into your vaguely-comic D&D pastiche. I'll get to that in a bit.

Burning Wheel folks have often asserted that the setting was always Middarmark, and that 2e just reflects a further refinement of those ideas. Fine. They can see it that way. But when I resolved to publish books for the Torchbearer Sagas license, the setting was defined by two sources, and neither of them was the as-yet-unpublished Middarmark. Torchbearer was the 1e rulebook plus Jared Sorensen's terrific expansion classes. Those classes were pure D&D pastiche as well. 

That was the game for several years, played weekly (or more) by me and my friends. If it was an accident left by a creative vacuum, so be it. It still means something to me.

## Nostalgia vs. Pastiche
Why am I so fixated on pastiche? There are concepts in RPG design like ***anti-canon*** which are super useful for explaining how settings interact with the table. And that's certainly a part of it— as a GM I have always struggled with setting paralysis, where trying to do a detailed setting justice can stop me from running the game. Torchbearer 1e was liberating in that there was nothing to tiptoe around.

But I'm not against detailed settings. Hell, I love them, and I'm guilty of pushing in that direction myself. TB1 seemed to invite me to do so, and TB2 needed me to live in Middarmark, or else do considerable work to leave it.

There was something much bigger going on with Torchbearer's D&D pastiche that was lost. It wasn't 100% nostalgia. Torchbearer's rules contain powerful value judgements: about the fallacies of heroism and power fantasy, about the nature of violence, and about wealth and success. These are also big concepts in D&D, but in Torchbearer they were each inverted. They were transformed into their opposite.

By being a thoughtfully-designed indie game set in a D&D pastiche, Torchbearer was not nostalgic, *it was a work of criticism*. And in our hobby, there could be no worthier subject of that criticism, and no greater vehicle for it than a game. Ask me why I love Planescape.

So here we're getting into real "forge" stuff (like I would know) — what's this game about? Because it seemed to me that this game was about viewing D&D through a lens of 50 years of the hobby's development and a deeper critical understanding of the themes at play. But I guess that was an accident? 

You can't just delete D&D from the formula. And for all its many problems, you can't really fix D&D's legacy by editing its mistakes. Many have tried. WotC's still trying. 

## Wither the Picaresque?
One of those value judgements that TB1 made was to make the adventurers into have-nots. They're not superheroes, and they're not Fantasy Rockstars (to borrow Duamn's term). I think almost all Torchbearer fans respond to that message, perhaps above all others.

But when you swap in TB1's non-setting for Middarmark's deep, historically informed setting, you get a different message. It's superficially the same. You do begin as have-nots.

Torchbearer 2e reverts to the zero-to-hero model of later D&D. Now our downtrodden adventurers are on the path to starting businesses and settlements, buying homes and gaining titles. They just need to work their way there. Instead of being outside the system, they're starting at the bottom and working their way up. The rules betray the designer's world view. 

This is reinforced by the constant reference to economies and economics, something I find to be exceptionally useless in an adventure game. I don't give a single shit about inflation in the local village, and the idea that a GM would be tabulating that stuff in the background is frankly infuriating.

All the new rules in Torchbearer 2 are a weird kind of capitalist virtue signaling and I don't care for it.
